Who built this

This tool was built by Brenden Mischewski, based in Porirua.

I work at Mischewski Consulting Limited, with a background in research and policy analysis in New Zealand.

Independent work

This is independent work. Nobody commissioned it, nobody paid for it, and it represents no client, council, government agency, political party or campaign. It is built entirely from published public data—every source is listed below. It uses no information obtained through any professional engagement. It is not connected to any submission being made under the Head Start process.

If I am ever engaged on work related to local government reorganisation, I will say so here and date it. If that engagement is for a party to a live proposal, I will retire this tool rather than continue running it alongside the work.

No position on amalgamation

This tool takes no position on amalgamation. It does not argue that any council should merge, or should not. It models the arithmetic consequences of combinations that users choose themselves, including combinations nobody has proposed and nobody wants. What follows from those numbers is a political judgement, and it is yours to make, not mine.

Anyone is welcome to use this tool, cite it or link to it, including in submissions and campaigns. Use by any group does not imply that I endorse that group’s position.

What this tool shows

The tool lets a user combine territorial authorities and compare five things: the population and land area of the combined council, how published 2024/25 average residential rates bills differ from a pooled average, each council’s historic net assets per resident at 30 June 2024 compared with the pooled position, each council’s historic total liabilities per resident compared with the pooled position, and where the combined population would live.

The dataset covers all 67 territorial authorities: 12 city councils, 53 district councils, Auckland Council and Chatham Islands Council. It excludes the 11 regional councils and their separate rates. Five territorial authorities formally carry regional-council functions; Chatham Islands performs some regional functions. Any two or more territorial and/or unitary authorities may submit a Head Start outline proposal except Auckland. Auckland remains in the dataset and can be modelled only as an expressly hypothetical comparison.

Why liabilities figures can differ from other reported debt figures

The tool compares council-only total liabilities per resident at 30 June 2024. Public reporting may instead use net debt, borrowings, debt after water-service transfers, a later date, or rating units rather than residents. Those choices can produce very different numbers without either figure being wrong.

For example, the tool records Taupō District Council's total liabilities as $231.909 million, or $5,495 per resident using the 2024 population of 42,200. RNZ reported separate 2026 modelling that put Taupō's net debt at about $900 per rating unit and the proposed Western Waikato authority at almost $9,000 per rating unit. The numerator, denominator, date and treatment of water services differ, so the figures are not directly comparable.

How the rate figures are calculated

The main result uses two figures published for each council in the New Zealand Taxpayers’ Union’s Ratepayers’ Report 2026: the published 2024/25 average residential rates bill and the report’s separately published household count, which the report attributes to Stats NZ. The household count is used as a weight. Population figures elsewhere in the tool are separate 2024 Stats NZ estimates published through the Department of Internal Affairs.

  1. Start with each selected council’s published average residential rates bill for 2024/25.
  2. Multiply that bill by the report’s published household count for the council.
  3. Add those weighted amounts across the selected councils.
  4. Divide by the combined published household count. This produces one household-weighted comparison.
  5. Compare that figure with each council’s own published average. The chart shows the dollar difference between those council-wide averages.

How the weighted amount differs from the actual residential total

For council i, let Aᵢ be the published average bill, Hᵢ the published household count, and Zᵢ the council-defined residential rating-unit count used to calculate that average.

Tool’s implied amount = Σ(Aᵢ × Hᵢ)

Actual residential rates and applicable charges = Σ(Aᵢ × Zᵢ)

Difference = Σ[Aᵢ × (Hᵢ − Zᵢ)]

The public report does not expose Zᵢ, or the underlying residential rates and applicable charges, so the exact dollar difference cannot be calculated from the published dataset. Total council rates revenue is available, but it includes non-residential rates and is not a valid substitute for the residential total.

This does not reproduce the Taxpayers’ Union’s residential-rates calculation. Its methodology defines an average bill as residential rates and applicable charges divided by council-defined residential rating units. The underlying rates, charges and residential rating-unit count—its X, Y and Z—are not exposed in the public report data. The separate household count used by this tool is therefore not necessarily the denominator used to calculate the published bill.

If a council has no comparable published bill, it is shown as “no data” and is not included in the weighted comparison. The output compares published council-wide averages. It does not reconstruct the residential rates pool, forecast an individual property, or determine the rating policy a merged council would adopt.

Historical water-continuity basis

The rates and balance-sheet comparisons retain water services exactly as they appeared in each published source at its stated date. Later transfers of water services, assets, liabilities, debt and customer billing to separate water organisations under Local Water Done Well are not modelled or substituted. Where water was already separately owned, billed or excluded at the source date, that published treatment remains.

This choice provides a consistent historical comparison through an in-flight national transition. It does not reconstruct every water organisation or claim to show a council’s post-reform legal balance sheet or a household’s current combined council-and-water charge.

Historic net assets per resident — 30 June 2024

For the balance-sheet comparison, the tool subtracts liabilities from assets for every selected council, pools those dollar amounts, and divides by the combined population. It then compares that pooled figure with each council’s historic net assets per resident. Water assets and liabilities held directly by councils at 30 June 2024 remain included; assets already held outside the council-only accounts remain excluded. Later water transfers are ignored. This is an accounting comparison, not a transfer payable to residents or a post-reform legal balance sheet.

Historic total liabilities per resident — 30 June 2024

The liabilities comparator adds the selected councils' council-only total liabilities and divides them by their combined population. It compares that pooled amount with each council's total liabilities per resident. It is not a measure of net debt, borrowings, debt per rating unit or the legal allocation of liabilities after amalgamation. The same historical water-continuity basis described above applies.

How elected representation is estimated

Before amalgamation: elected representatives are the councillors on the selected territorial authorities plus the regional councillors estimated to overlap the selected area. Mayors are shown separately. The territorial and regional figures come from the 2025 Local Authority Election Statistics, with Tauranga’s current 2024–28 arrangement.

When every territorial authority in a region is selected, the model includes every regional councillor. For a partial-region proposal, it estimates the number of overlapping regional councillors from the selected councils’ share of the regional population. This is an approximation because regional constituency boundaries are not mapped in the tool.

After amalgamation: the illustrative governing body has approximately one elected representative per 25,000 residents, rounded to a whole seat. It has a minimum of six and a maximum of 20 representatives, and at least one representative for every former council area. The mayor is additional. Governing-body seats are allocated between former council areas by population using the largest-remainder method.

Community Councils: the model applies the broad approach illustrated by MartinJenkins in July 2026: approximately one Community Council member per 15,000 residents, with at least four members for every former council area. If a former council area receives more governing-body representatives than its Community Council would otherwise contain, the Community Council expands to accommodate them.

Governing-body representatives are assumed to serve on both the unitary governing body and their Community Council. The bars therefore compare elected roles; their categories should not be added together to estimate the number of unique people. These calculations are illustrative, not a recommendation or forecast. Final boundaries, wards, Māori representation and membership would be determined through detailed design and statutory representation processes.

Why the Wellington financial report differs

The July 2026 Wellington/Wairarapa financial analysis removes water activities from council forecasts, except Kāpiti Coast’s in-house water services. Its rates, debt and financial-resilience findings therefore use a different, post-transfer financial perimeter and are not directly comparable with this tool’s historical water-continuity figures.

What these numbers are not

The indicative rate figures are a household-weighted comparison of published council-wide averages. They are not a redistribution across the actual residential rating base and do not model:

  • the residential rates, applicable charges and council-defined residential rating-unit counts used to produce each published average;
  • transition and implementation costs;
  • debt harmonisation or ring-fencing across merging entities;
  • service-level changes, up or down;
  • differential rating, targeted rates, uniform charges or remission policy in a merged entity;
  • efficiency gains or losses;
  • property values or the circumstances of an individual property;
  • the separate regional-council rates layer;
  • later transfers of water services, assets, liabilities, debt or customer billing to separate water organisations;
  • a uniform treatment of water where it was already separately owned, billed or excluded at the source date;
  • the location, condition or remaining life of council assets; or
  • any decision an amalgamated council would actually make about its rating structure.

These are not forecasts. They show a possible direction when published council-wide averages are compared using a separate household weight. A real amalgamation would set a new rating structure, and the size, timing and direction of property-level changes could differ. Treat the outputs as a starting point for a question, not an answer to one.

Auckland is not evidence either way

The tool does not use the 2010 Auckland amalgamation as evidence for or against current proposals. Its post-merger rates and efficiency outcomes are contested, and this model does not attempt to resolve that literature.
