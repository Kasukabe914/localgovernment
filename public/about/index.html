Who built this

This tool was built by Brenden Mischewski, based in Whitby, Porirua.

I work in public sector policy and advisory through Mischewski Consulting Limited, with a background in research and policy analysis in New Zealand. I built this because communities are being asked to assess merger options, and the distributional arithmetic underneath those options is not easy to see.

Independent work

This is independent work. Nobody commissioned it, nobody paid for it, and it represents no client, council, government agency, political party or campaign. It is built entirely from published public data—every source is listed below. It uses no information obtained through any professional engagement. It is not connected to any submission being made under the Head Start process.

If I am ever engaged on work related to local government reorganisation, I will say so here and date it. If that engagement is for a party to a live proposal, I will retire this tool rather than continue running it alongside the work.

No position on amalgamation

This tool takes no position on amalgamation. It does not argue that any council should merge, or should not. It models the arithmetic consequences of combinations that users choose themselves, including combinations nobody has proposed and nobody wants. What follows from those numbers is a political judgement, and it is yours to make, not mine.

Anyone is welcome to use this tool, cite it or link to it, including in submissions and campaigns. Use by any group does not imply that I endorse that group’s position.

What this tool shows

The tool lets a user combine territorial authorities and compare four things: the population and land area of the combined council, how today’s published average residential rates bills differ from a pooled average, each council’s net assets per resident compared with the pooled position, and where the combined population would live.

The dataset covers all 67 territorial authorities: 12 city councils, 53 district councils, Auckland Council and Chatham Islands Council. It excludes the 11 regional councils and their separate rates. Five territorial authorities formally carry regional-council functions; Chatham Islands performs some regional functions. Auckland remains in the national dataset but is locked out of Head Start combinations.

How the rate figures are calculated

The main result compares each council’s published average residential rates bill using the Ratepayers’ Report’s separately published household count as a weight. It does not divide total council revenue by population and call that a household bill.

  1. Start with each selected council’s published average residential rates bill for 2024/25.
  2. Multiply that bill by the report’s published household count for the council.
  3. Add those weighted amounts across the selected councils.
  4. Divide by the combined published household count. This produces one household-weighted comparison.
  5. Compare that figure with each council’s own published average. The chart shows the dollar difference between those council-wide averages.

This does not reproduce the Taxpayers’ Union’s residential-rates calculation. Its methodology defines an average bill as residential rates and applicable charges divided by council-defined residential rating units. The underlying rates, charges and residential rating-unit count—its X, Y and Z—are not exposed in the public report data. The separate household count used by this tool is therefore not necessarily the denominator used to calculate the published bill.

If a council has no comparable published bill, it is shown as “no data” and is not included in the weighted comparison. The output compares published council-wide averages. It does not reconstruct the residential rates pool, forecast an individual property, or determine the rating policy a merged council would adopt.

Net assets per resident

For the balance-sheet comparison, the tool subtracts liabilities from assets for every selected council, pools those dollar amounts, and divides by the combined population. It then compares that pooled figure with each council’s current net assets per resident. This is an accounting comparison, not a transfer payable to residents.

What these numbers are not

The indicative rate figures are a household-weighted comparison of published council-wide averages. They are not a redistribution across the actual residential rating base and do not model:

  • the residential rates, applicable charges and council-defined residential rating-unit counts used to produce each published average;
  • transition and implementation costs;
  • debt harmonisation or ring-fencing across merging entities;
  • service-level changes, up or down;
  • differential rating, targeted rates, uniform charges or remission policy in a merged entity;
  • efficiency gains or losses;
  • property values or the circumstances of an individual property;
  • the separate regional-council rates layer;
  • the location, condition or remaining life of council assets; or
  • any decision an amalgamated council would actually make about its rating structure.

These are not forecasts. They show a possible direction when published council-wide averages are compared using a separate household weight. A real amalgamation would set a new rating structure, and the size, timing and direction of property-level changes could differ. Treat the outputs as a starting point for a question, not an answer to one.

Auckland is not evidence either way

The tool does not use the 2010 Auckland amalgamation as evidence for or against current proposals. Its post-merger rates and efficiency outcomes are contested, and this model does not attempt to resolve that literature.
