How the numbers are made

This page documents every input, calculation and assumption in the tool so an analyst can reproduce the model rather than infer it from interface copy. The tool itself is at the main page.

Known data caveats, stated plainly. (1) The Ratepayers' Report column labelled "Households" behaves as a dwelling count, not occupied households: nine bach-heavy districts imply under two residents per dwelling (Thames-Coromandel 1.2), which is impossible for occupied households. The tool therefore calls it "residential properties" throughout. (2) Chatham Islands is absent from both the Ratepayers' Report and the rating-units dataset; its property count is an estimate and it shows "no data" on the bill basis. (3) Western Bay of Plenty, Westland and Waitaki declined or did not supply an average bill. (4) The DIA and Ratepayers' Report population figures disagree by up to 2.8% for some councils (different vintages); the tool uses DIA population everywhere. (5) Rating-unit counts mix confirmed actuals (51 councils) with older, projected, draft and proxy figures; the status is flagged per row in the CSV and on the comparator rows in the tool.

2. GST treatment

Every dollar figure shown in the tool is GST-inclusive. Published average residential bills are already GST-inclusive; rates revenue is reported GST-exclusive in council accounts, so revenue-derived figures are multiplied by 1.15. The CSV supplies revenue GST-exclusive, exactly as reported; apply ×1.15 to reproduce the tool's figures.

3. Calculations

Let R be a council's rates revenue for the chosen year (× 1.15), P its 2024 population, D its residential property count, U its rating units, and B its published average residential bill.

The change shown for each council is blend − own value: an immediate, fully harmonised redistribution of today's revenue. Blends require at least two members with usable data; members without data are excluded from the blend and shown as "no data". The optional "trim" slider multiplies the revenue-derived take by (1 − s%) and is a hypothetical illustration, not an evidenced savings model. It never applies to the published-bill basis.

Regional mandate: a group's combined 2024 population divided by the total population of the territorial authorities in the region(s) its members span. The greater-than-50% threshold reflects reporting that the reform lets a coalition holding a majority of a region's population lodge a proposal covering the whole region, including objecting councils. This is as reported, not legal advice.

Population share bars show each member's share of the merged population. They are not a model of voting power: wards, councillor numbers and community-board arrangements are set by each proposal and are not modelled here.

4. Scenario assumptions

The opening map ("Where talks stand, mid-July") is a snapshot of reporting as at 24 July 2026 (Local Democracy Reporting / The Post (Justin Wong), RNZ, and The Spinoff, Joel MacManus, 24 July 2026), resolved into one group per council. Where reporting showed a council backing competing options, or objecting to a grouping it appears in, the one-group-per-council data model cannot represent that; the map is an illustrative scenario, not a statement of any council's agreement. Other presets (a single Wellington authority; the ten-council Waikato grouping; one council per region) are illustrative constructions for comparison.

5. What is not modelled

  • Transition costs and phasing. Establishment costs, rates caps, phased harmonisation, legacy differentials, targeted rates and ring-fenced legacy debt all defer, smooth or alter the redistribution shown. MartinJenkins' July 2026 estimates (e.g. ~$269m to establish a Wellington metro authority) illustrate the scale. The tool's figures are the end-state redistribution, applied instantly; no real amalgamation has worked that way.
  • Governance design and representation. Wards, Māori wards, councillor numbers, community boards or community councils, and local voice arrangements.
  • The regional-council layer. All figures are territorial-council only. The 11 regional councils' separate rates are excluded, so no figure is a household's complete rates bill; the four existing unitary authorities (Gisborne, Nelson, Tasman, Marlborough) already include regional functions and are not strictly like-for-like.
  • Property-level incidence. Rates are levied on land or capital value with differentials and targeted rates; averages hide a wide spread within every district.
  • Population growth after 2024. Forecast-year figures use the 2024 population, which overstates per-person figures by up to about 8% in the fastest-growing districts. Water services reform, debt positions, service levels and asset condition also sit outside the model.

6. Reproducing the model

  1. Download the CSV.
  2. Gross up the two revenue columns by ×1.15.
  3. Apply the formulas in section 3 to any set of councils.
  4. Compare against the tool with the same set selected. Every figure should match to the dollar.
